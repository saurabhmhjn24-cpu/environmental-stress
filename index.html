References & calculation methods

Heat Index / feels like

Temperature is converted to °F: F = 1.8T + 32. Initial estimate: H = 0.5[F + 61 + 1.2(F − 68) + 0.094R], where R is relative humidity (%). If (H + F)/2 ≥80°F, the Rothfusz regression is used:

H = −42.379 + 2.04901523F + 10.14333127R − 0.22475541FR − 0.00683783F² − 0.05481717R² + 0.00122874F²R + 0.00085282FR² − 0.00000199F²R².

For R<13% and F=80–112°F, subtract [(13−R)/4]√[(17−|F−95|)/17]. For R>85% and F=80–87°F, add [(R−85)/10][(87−F)/5]. Convert back with (H−32)×5/9. This is a screening estimate, not a measured body temperature.

Calculation basis: the displayed “feels like” temperature uses the US National Weather Service Rothfusz Heat Index regression (temperature + relative humidity). The green/yellow/red chart background retains this application's existing Safe/Caution/Danger boundary curves. The Safe→Caution boundary is T = 40.1247562 − 0.287219702RH + 0.00195590150RH² − 0.00000452294349RH³. The Caution→Danger boundary is T = min(42.5, 52.9675892 − 0.468153228RH + 0.00421088398RH² − 0.0000162471061RH³), with 42.5°C used below RH 29.137%. These boundary curves are local decision-aid equations in this application and are not official NWS alert thresholds. For occupational heat-stress control, NIOSH recommends WBGT for exposure limits; Heat Index is a screening alternative when WBGT is unavailable and does not account fully for workload, radiant heat or clothing/PPE.

Calculation basis: Wind chill = 13.12 + 0.6215T − 11.37V0.16 + 0.3965TV0.16, where T is °C and V is wind speed in km/h. In this app it is applied at or below 10°C and above 4.8 km/h, matching the NWS definition range. Low-to-Extreme risk bands and approximate frostbite times follow Environment Canada; the >0°C “Minimal” band is an app display category.

Indian AQI

Each pollutant sub-index is calculated by linear interpolation between CPCB concentration breakpoints: I = I_low + (I_high − I_low) × (C − C_low)/(C_high − C_low). Overall AQI is the rounded maximum sub-index. At least three pollutants, including PM₂.₅ or PM₁₀, are required by this implementation; values above its tables are capped at 500.

Model estimates use 24-hour averages for PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, NO₂ and SO₂ (at least 18 hourly values), and 8-hour averages for O₃ and CO (at least six). CO is converted from µg/m³ to mg/m³. A configured CPCB service is preferred when a station within 25 km has a reading no older than three hours. Otherwise Open-Meteo modelled pollutants are used; the app estimates Indian AQI rather than displaying US AQI.

UV, visibility & elevation

Live location readings request hourly UV Index and visibility from Open-Meteo, selecting the hour matching its current weather timestamp. UV and visibility are model estimates. Elevation is terrain height, converted to feet with metres × 3.28084; it is not a GPS altitude measurement.

These extra parameters are not requested for selected historical/future times or manually entered weather values and may show unavailable. Automatic chart selection uses cold below 20°C; this switch is an app display choice, not a medical threshold. Missing wind uses air temperature without wind-chill correction; missing humidity prevents heat assessment.

Visibility display thresholds

Visibility: <1 km fog-range; 1–<5 km reduced; ≥5 km good viewing distance. The 5 km division is an app display threshold. Wind gusts are displayed separately from sustained wind and are not substituted into wind chill.

Actions, clothing guidance & forecast timeline

The next 12 forecast hours use Open-Meteo temperature, humidity, sustained wind and UV data. Thermal categories use the same equations as the chart; automatic mode switches below 20°C. The AQI forecast applies the existing Indian AQI interpolation and rolling averages to hourly model pollutant forecasts; unavailable pollutants remain unavailable. Weather and AQI are separate model estimates.

Weather age uses the provider timestamp; retrieval time is shown in the update-time tooltip. Live weather older than 90 minutes is flagged in Recommended actions. A forecast validity time is not its model-run issue time. Manual values are not verified measurements.

Recommended actions use the displayed conditions, with higher-category hazards listed first. This ordering is a presentation choice, not a validated combined risk score. Clothing suggestions are general layering guidance; garment insulation, wetness, workload and individual factors prevent an exact clothing count or safe duration being inferred from air temperature.

High-altitude preventive guidance

High-altitude preventive guidance
Use this section when planning ascent to or stay at higher elevations.

1. Gradual ascent

  • Do not go too high too fast. Above 2,700 m (about 9,000 ft), keep the gain in sleeping altitude to no more than about 500 m (1,650 ft) per day.
  • Allow an additional rest/acclimatization day for every 1,000–1,500 m (3,300–4,900 ft) of ascent.
  • Do not ascend further if symptoms compatible with acute mountain sickness develop. Resume ascent only after symptoms have completely resolved; descend if symptoms persist or worsen.

2. Staged acclimatization schedule

2,700–3,600 m
(9,000–12,000 ft) · 6 days
Days 1–2: rest except short, easy level walks.
Days 3–4: slow walk 1.5–3 km; avoid steep climbs.
Days 5–6: walk up to 5 km and climb up to about 300 m at a slow pace.
>3,600–4,500 m
(12,000–15,000 ft) · 4 days
Days 1–2: slow walk 1.5–3 km; avoid steep climbs.
Day 3: slow walk with climb up to about 300 m.
Day 4: climb about 300 m at a slow pace without extra load.
>4,500 m
(>15,000 ft) · 4 days
Use the same gradual activity progression as the preceding 4-day stage. Avoid rapid further ascent and excessive exertion.

Acclimatization varies between individuals and continues beyond the staged period. After a prolonged stay at low altitude, re-acclimatization is required; after more than about 4 weeks away, repeat the full staged schedule.

3. Activity during early acclimatization

  • Keep activity light during the first 48 hours and increase exertion gradually.
  • Avoid unusually strenuous exercise during the first week, even if you feel well.
  • Physical fitness does not eliminate the risk of altitude illness.

4. Hydration, nutrition and substances

  • Maintain normal hydration; cold, dry air increases insensible fluid loss. Drink regularly, but do not force excessive fluids.
  • Eat regular meals with adequate carbohydrate intake during ascent and acclimatization.
  • Avoid alcohol during the first 48 hours at altitude and avoid sedative or respiratory-depressant medicines unless specifically prescribed.

5. Cold and UV protection

  • Use layered, dry clothing and wind protection; wind can markedly increase heat loss.
  • Protect exposed skin, hands, feet, head and face as cold intensity increases.
  • UV exposure increases with elevation. Use protective clothing, a broad-brimmed hat, UV-protective eyewear and broad-spectrum sunscreen; strengthen protection when the UV Index is 3 or above.

6. When to postpone ascent or descend

  • Postpone ascent during an acute illness, especially significant respiratory or gastrointestinal illness.
  • Stop further ascent for new headache with nausea, dizziness, unusual fatigue or sleep disturbance after gaining altitude.
  • Descend and seek urgent medical help for confusion, loss of coordination, severe breathlessness at rest, rapidly worsening symptoms or marked reduction in exercise tolerance.

7. Preventive medication

Medication to prevent altitude illness may be appropriate when rapid ascent is unavoidable or in people with a prior history of altitude illness. It should be considered after medical assessment rather than self-started.

Sources: staged schedule adapted from Medical Memorandum No. 189 (2017), Problems of High Altitude; general ascent and prevention principles cross-checked against CDC Yellow Book 2026 and Wilderness Medical Society 2024 guidance. See References & methods.
